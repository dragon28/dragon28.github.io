A nanometre is a billionth of a metre. Nanotechnology works with structures between about 1 and 100 nanometres, where materials start to behave differently: colours change, surfaces become more reactive, and quantum effects show up ([nano.gov](https://www.nano.gov/nanotech-101/what/definition)). Most of this work happens in clean rooms, but some of it can be reached from a garage or kitchen. Here are five ways hobbyists do it.

<!--more-->

## 1. Graphene from a kitchen blender

Graphene is a single layer of carbon atoms, and its discovery won the [2010 Nobel Prize in Physics](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2010/summary/). Andre Geim and Konstantin Novoselov famously peeled it off graphite with sticky tape. In 2014, a team led by Jonathan Coleman at Trinity College Dublin showed that you can also make it in bulk with shear mixing. Their [paper in *Nature Materials*](https://www.nature.com/articles/nmat3944) included a demonstration with an ordinary kitchen blender, graphite powder, water and dishwashing liquid. The detergent coats the flakes and keeps them from sticking back together.

**What you get:** a grey liquid holding few-layer graphene flakes. You can paint it onto paper to make simple conductive traces.

## 2. A scanning tunneling microscope that sees atoms

A scanning tunneling microscope (STM) moves a very sharp metal tip a few atoms above a surface. It measures the tiny "tunneling" current that leaks across the gap ([how it works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scanning_tunneling_microscope)). Engineer Dan Berard built [one at home](https://dberard.com/home-built-stm/) from piezo discs, a vibration-isolation stack and custom electronics. It imaged the individual rows of atoms in graphite. This is the most demanding project here, but it shows that atomic resolution is no longer limited to professional labs.

## 3. Ferrofluid: liquid magnets

[Ferrofluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferrofluid) is oil holding magnetite particles about 10 nanometres across, each coated with a surfactant. The particles are so small that they never settle. Bring a magnet close and the liquid rises into spikes. Ready-made ferrofluid is cheap and is the safest way to start. Put it in a sealed glass vial and play with a magnet from outside the vial.

## 4. The 1,600-year-old nano glass

The Roman [Lycurgus Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycurgus_Cup), from the 4th century AD, looks green when light reflects off it and glows red when light shines through it. The glass contains gold and silver nanoparticles about 50–100 nanometres across. Their size controls which colours of light they scatter, an effect called *surface plasmon resonance*. Medieval stained-glass makers used the same effect without knowing why it worked. Colloidal gold demonstrations, often run in university outreach labs, recreate this ruby-red colour in a test tube.

## 5. Soap films: nanometres you can see

A soap bubble's wall is only a few hundred nanometres thick, and it thins as the liquid drains. Light bouncing off the front and back of the film interferes, which produces the swirling colours. Shortly before a film pops, it can turn black. At that point it is thinner than a few tens of nanometres. Hold a soap film on a wire loop in front of a dark background with soft white light. You are watching a nanoscale film change thickness in real time.

## Safety first

> - **Never breathe in nanopowders.** Work with liquids and pastes, wear gloves, and wipe up spills while they're wet. Don't sweep dry powder.
> - Keep ferrofluid sealed. It stains skin, fabric and furniture permanently.
> - Chemical syntheses such as colloidal gold or silver use reagents that stain, irritate or are toxic. Only do them with lab supervision, eye protection and proper waste disposal.
> - Keep electronics and mains power away from liquids.
